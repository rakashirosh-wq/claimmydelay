EU261, explained plainly

The actual rules, not a sales pitch. Sourced from the regulation and EU case law.

What it covers

EU Regulation 261/2004 sets compensation for passengers facing denied boarding, flight cancellation, or long delays — on flights departing an EU/EEA airport (any airline), or arriving in the EU/EEA on an EU-based airline.

The 3-hour rule

Delay compensation only kicks in at 3+ hours late at your final destination — not departure delay. A flight that leaves 4 hours late but makes up time and lands only 2 hours late doesn't qualify. This is based on the Sturgeon ruling by the Court of Justice of the EU (2009), which extended delay compensation to match cancellation compensation.

"Extraordinary circumstances" — the part airlines dispute most

Airlines don't owe compensation if the disruption was caused by something outside their control: severe weather, air traffic control strikes (when not the airline's own staff), security threats, or political instability. They generally do owe compensation for technical/mechanical faults, crew shortages, and most strikes by their own staff — even though airlines sometimes dispute this.

Verified · checked Jun 2026 The Court of Justice of the EU has repeatedly ruled that technical problems are generally part of normal airline operations, not "extraordinary," unless caused by something outside the airline's control like a manufacturing defect or sabotage.

What it doesn't cover

EU261 doesn't apply to delays under 3 hours, cancellations with 14+ days' notice, or flights entirely outside EU jurisdiction (e.g. a US domestic flight). It also doesn't cover indirect costs like a missed hotel booking — only the fixed compensation amount and, separately, "duty of care" provisions (meals, hotel, communication) during the disruption itself.
